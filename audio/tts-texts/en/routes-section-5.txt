Freight in the United States swings hard with the seasons. Produce season, holiday season, harvest — all of it moves rates, load availability and road conditions. Experienced dispatchers plan around these patterns and earn more for it.

Spring: March, April, May. Produce season begins. Florida and California start shipping fruit and vegetables north and east in volume, and reefer rates climb twenty to forty percent. Construction season starts in the northern states, so flatbed demand rises. This is one of the best earning periods of the year. Florida ships tomatoes, strawberries and citrus into the Northeast. California ships lettuce and strawberries to the Midwest and East Coast. Building materials — lumber, steel — move north. Reefer rates run $2.50 to $3.50 at the peak. Watch the weather: tornadoes in Tornado Alley across Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas cause delays.

Summer: June, July, August. Peak produce season. California ships at maximum volume and construction runs everywhere. But the heat creates problems: reefer units work at their limit, drivers tire faster, and tires blow more often on hot asphalt. Hurricane season starts in June and threatens the Gulf Coast. The Central Valley peaks with almonds, grapes and peaches. Beverage season lifts dry van demand for water and drinks. Heat above 110 degrees means reefer trouble and roughly five times the tire failures. And back-to-school in August raises consumer goods demand.

Fall: September, October, November. The most profitable period in freight. Harvest season across the Midwest moves grain, corn and soybeans. Holiday season begins as retailers prepare for Black Friday and Christmas. California produce continues. Rates hit their annual peak in October and November — this is when a dispatcher earns the most. Harvest moves grain out of Iowa, Illinois, Indiana and Ohio to ports and elevators. Holiday freight fills retail warehouses before Black Friday, and Amazon's peak season runs October through December with enormous demand. Dry van rates reach $2.50 to $3.00 and above, the yearly high. First frosts in the Midwest and Northeast change reefer settings.

Winter: December, January, February. A hard stretch. Holiday freight continues through December, but after New Year comes the January slump, the slowest month of the year, with rates down twenty to thirty percent. Snow and ice in the northern states cause delays and dangerous conditions. But Florida produce starts in January, and emergency freight caused by winter storms can pay very well. December is the last push of holiday freight at high rates. January bottoms out: little freight, snow and ice on I-70, I-80 and I-90, frequent closures and delays. Florida produce runs January through March, lifting reefer rates out of the state. And de-icing salt moves urgently into the northern states.

The advice: send your drivers south — Florida, Texas — in January and February.
