Five techniques, each proven by thousands of dispatchers in real calls. Combine them depending on the situation.

Technique one: never accept the first offer. Say: "Can you do any better on the rate?" The broker's first number is a starting point, not a final one. That single question adds fifty to two hundred dollars to a load. Brokers expect negotiation — if you do not negotiate, they mark you as a beginner and lowball you in future.

Technique two: anchoring. Name your price first. Say: "I need $2,800 on this lane to make it work." Whoever says a number first sets the frame. If you said $2,800, the haggling happens around that. If the broker said $2,200, you are climbing up from their anchor. Name a price fifteen to twenty percent above your actual target.

Technique three: silence after the offer. When the broker names a rate, stay quiet for five to seven seconds. Silence is a powerful tool. A broker who names a number and hears nothing gets uncomfortable, and often raises it themselves: "Well, I might be able to do $2,500." Do not fill the pause. Let the broker fill it with money.

Technique four: cite the data. Say: "DAT shows this lane averaging $2.45 per mile this week." Data is your strongest argument, because a broker cannot argue with market statistics. Check DAT RateView before every call. When you speak in numbers, the broker recognizes a professional and stops trying to underpay.

Technique five: the alternative. Say: "I have another load paying $2.60 on a similar lane. Can you match that?" Even without a specific alternative, mentioning competition creates pressure — the broker understands you are not dependent on them. One caution: do not bluff too obviously. If they ask for details, have at least a rough answer ready.

Those five are the foundation of any negotiation. Always ask if they can do better. Name your price first. Use silence. Speak in DAT numbers. And create the impression that you have options.

Practice them on every call. It feels awkward at first, but after ten or twenty calls it becomes automatic. Each technique adds fifty to two hundred dollars per load. Across two hundred loads a year, that is ten to forty thousand dollars of additional income.

Negotiation is not talent. It is a skill you train.
