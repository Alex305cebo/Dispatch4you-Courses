The United States splits into five key freight regions, each with its own character. A dispatcher needs to know which freight dominates where, what rates to expect, and how the seasons move them.

The Northeast. The most densely populated region, with enormous consumer volume but difficult logistics — narrow roads, congestion, and truck restrictions in the cities. This is the economic center of the country, more than 55 million people, and New York, Boston and Philadelphia generate constant demand. Average rate per mile: $2.50 to $3.50, above the national average because the work is harder. Main freight: consumer goods, electronics, pharmaceuticals. The ports of New York and Boston bring container imports. In winter, snow means delays and higher rates. And the tolls bite: the New Jersey Turnpike and the George Washington Bridge run fifteen to a hundred dollars or more for a truck.

The Southeast. A fast-growing region with serious logistics infrastructure. Atlanta is one of the largest transport hubs in the country, and Florida is a key produce market. The Southeast is booming as companies move warehouses and distribution centers out of the expensive Northeast, and Atlanta has become the second most important logistics hub after Chicago. Average rate per mile: $2.00 to $2.80. Atlanta sits where I-75, I-85 and I-20 cross, so freight runs in every direction. Florida produce season, January through April, sends rates out of Florida sharply up. The Port of Savannah handles growing container volume, up fifteen percent a year. And hurricane season, June through November, brings emergency freight at high rates.

The Midwest. The heart of American industry and agriculture. Chicago is the largest transport hub in the country, with steady demand year round — more freight passes through Chicago than through any other city. The region builds cars in Detroit, grows crops in Iowa and Kansas, and manufactures in Ohio and Indiana. Average rate per mile: $1.80 to $2.50. Chicago sits on I-90, I-94, I-80 and I-55, which reaches the whole country. Harvest season, September through November, moves grain, corn and soybeans on flatbeds and hoppers. Detroit runs auto parts on just-in-time delivery for the plants. And winter brings heavy snow and blizzards, with delays on I-80 and I-90.

The Southwest. Texas is the second largest state and one of the main freight markets in the country. Dallas, Houston and San Antonio are powerful hubs with enormous volume. Texas is effectively its own economy, the size of a small country. Houston is the oil and gas capital. Dallas-Fort Worth is a logistics giant. Laredo is the largest crossing point with Mexico. Average rate per mile: $1.80 to $2.60. Dallas-Fort Worth is the third largest freight hub in the country. Laredo carries forty percent of all U.S.-Mexico trade. Houston brings oil, gas, chemicals, and the second largest port in the United States. And summer heat above 110 degrees demands attention on reefer loads.

The West Coast. California is the largest economy of any state. The ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach handle forty percent of all U.S. imports, and produce season here runs nearly year round. This is the gateway for Asian imports and the largest agricultural producer in the country — California alone grows more than half of all U.S. fruit and vegetables. Rates run $2.00 to $3.00 outbound but only $1.50 to $2.00 inbound. Los Angeles and Long Beach move container freight. The Central Valley ships produce all year: lettuce, strawberries, almonds. And CARB compliance means only trucks from 2010 or newer may operate in California.

Mountain and Plains. The least populated region, with long distances between cities and little freight for the return trip — a classic deadhead zone, but with real opportunity in season. Distances are enormous, towns are few, and backhauls often do not exist. During harvest, though — grain, sugar beets — rates can be very attractive. Average rate per mile: $1.50 to $2.00, heavily seasonal. Deadhead frequently runs 200 miles or more to the next load. Winter brings extreme conditions, thirty below and road closures. The advice: plan your exit from this region before you enter it, and find the backhaul first.
