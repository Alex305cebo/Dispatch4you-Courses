Eight problems come back again and again. Learn the routine for each and you will handle ninety-five percent of what real work throws at you.

One: a breakdown. Critical level. Step by step. Make sure the driver is safe — hazards on, triangles out. Call ERS, emergency road service, and have that number in your phone before you need it. Notify the broker immediately with a new ETA. If the repair will take four hours or more, start looking for a backup truck. Document everything: photos, timestamps, repair receipts.

Two: cargo damage. Critical level. What you do in the first thirty minutes decides whether insurance pays. The driver does not leave. Photograph all the damage. The receiver notes the damage on the BOL. The driver signs with the notation "damage noted at delivery." You call the broker immediately and notify the insurer the same day. Do not admit fault — that is the insurer's call.

Three: detention. Serious level. Industry data puts average detention at three and a half hours. At $75 an hour after two hours of free time, that is $112 lost, and you lose it entirely if it is not documented. The driver records arrival time with a GPS-stamped photo. After two hours you notify the broker in writing: driver in detention, arrived at this time, detention clock started. Then send the invoice with the timestamps.

Four: TONU, truck ordered not used. The broker cancels after your driver already rolled. Standard compensation is $150 to $350. Screenshot the cancellation the moment it happens. Check the rate con for a TONU clause. Send the broker a written demand with an invoice. Without that clause in the rate con, collecting is close to impossible.

Five: the broker is not paying, forty-five days or more. Send a payment follow-up with a copy of the invoice and the POD. Call the broker's accounting department. At sixty days with no result, file a claim against the broker's surety bond through FMCSA. Above five thousand dollars, talk to a transportation attorney. To prevent all this, check the broker through your factoring company before you book.

Six: the driver cannot find the place. Check the address on the rate con. Find the dock and gate number. Call the shipper contact listed on the rate con. Use Google Maps satellite view.

Seven: weather and road closures. Driver safety is priority one — if it is unsafe, you stop. Notify the broker with a new ETA. Adverse driving conditions legally give the driver two extra hours of drive time. Make sure it is marked in the ELD.

Eight: the driver wants to quit. You listen. You acknowledge the problem. You offer something concrete — a premium load tomorrow, or a better lane. Losing a good driver costs about five thousand dollars in lost revenue, plus the time it takes to find a replacement.
