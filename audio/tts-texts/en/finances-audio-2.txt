Cost per mile. The most important number in trucking.

According to ATRI, the American Transportation Research Institute, average CPM for a Class 8 truck in 2025 was $2.26 a mile including driver pay. Without driver pay, for owner-operators, it runs $1.27 to $1.85 depending on the age of the truck and the region.

Why does this number matter so much? A broker offers $2.50 a mile. Is that good or bad? If your CPM is $1.85, you make 65 cents a mile — excellent. If your CPM is $2.60, you lose 10 cents on every mile. Over 10,000 miles a month, that is a thousand dollars of loss. Knowing your CPM is the difference between profit and bankruptcy.

Here is where the money goes, at 10,000 miles a month.

Fuel is the biggest line: thirty-five to forty percent of costs. At $3.80 a gallon and 6 miles per gallon, that is 63 cents a mile, or $6,500 a month. Fuel cards save 5 to 50 cents a gallon. Better driving habits save another five to ten percent.

Insurance runs $12,000 to $25,000 a year per truck. New authorities pay more, because they have no history. A bad CSA score adds twenty to fifty percent. Cargo insurance of $100,000 is standard; high-value freight needs $250,000 to $500,000.

Maintenance runs 15 to 25 cents a mile. A new truck, 10 to 15 cents. A truck five years or older, 20 to 30 cents. Ten years or older, 30 to 50 cents and climbing. The rule: set aside 10 to 12 cents a mile in a maintenance fund. Preventive maintenance costs about $2,500 a month but prevents breakdowns that cost three to ten thousand. It pays for itself ten to twenty times over.

The rest: the truck payment, roughly 33 cents a mile if the truck is financed. Tolls and permits, 5 to 8 cents. Technology — phone, TMS, load board — about 2 cents.

Add it up at 10,000 miles and CPM lands at $1.41 to $1.49 without driver pay. At an RPM of $2.50, that is about $10,000 a month in profit before taxes.
