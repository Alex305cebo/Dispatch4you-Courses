Types of dispatchers, and what each one earns. There are three, with different terms and different ceilings.

Type one: the in-house dispatcher. You work directly for a trucking company as a W2 employee, running the company's fleet, usually five to twenty trucks per dispatcher. With three to seven years of experience, $54,000 to $64,000. As a dispatch manager, eight years and up, $58,000 to $90,000 and beyond. The upside: stability and benefits — health insurance, a 401(k), paid vacation. The downside: a hard income ceiling and dependence on one employer.

Type two: the independent dispatcher. You work for yourself as a 1099 contractor. You find your own clients — owner-operators — and dispatch their trucks for a percentage, usually five to ten percent of gross revenue, with seven percent as the standard. Here is the math. One truck grossing $200,000 a year pays you $14,000 — not enough to live on. Three trucks, $42,000, the minimum to start. Five trucks, $70,000, a comfortable income. Ten trucks, $140,000, an excellent one. Fifteen trucks, $210,000, the top of what a solo dispatcher manages. The upside: no ceiling, freedom, a flexible schedule. The downside: no stability, no benefits, you find your own clients, and the job runs twenty-four seven.

Type three: the dispatch service owner. You have opened your own business, hired other dispatchers, and you manage a team. The business model at fifty trucks: revenue around $700,000 a year, costs for five dispatchers and tools around $330,000, leaving roughly $370,000 in profit. Twenty trucks produces $100,000 to $150,000. A hundred trucks produces $500,000 to $700,000.

The typical path. Years one and two: in-house dispatcher, learning and building experience. Years two and three: go independent, find your first three to five clients. Years three through five: scale toward fifty trucks, earning $70,000 to $140,000. Year five and beyond: own the dispatch service, run a team, and earn $100,000 to $500,000 and up.
