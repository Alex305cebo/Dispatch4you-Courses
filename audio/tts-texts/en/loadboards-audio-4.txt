Reading rates. The core dispatcher skill.

Rate per mile is the main metric: divide the total rate by the miles and you have your price per mile.

Minimums by equipment. Dry van: minimum $1.80 a mile, good is $2.20, excellent is $2.50 and up. Reefer: minimum $2.00, good is $2.50, excellent is $3.00 and up. Flatbed: minimum $2.20, good is $2.70, excellent is $3.20 and up.

Account for deadhead. If the pickup is fifty miles away, those miles come out of your profit. Example: an 800-mile load at $2,000, plus fifty miles of deadhead, is 850 miles total. Two thousand divided by 850 is $2.35 a mile, not $2.50.

Weigh the backhaul. If it is easy to find freight out of the delivery area, you can take a slightly lower rate. If the backhaul looks bad, ask for more.

Watch the season. Peak is September through December, when rates run high. The slow season is January and February, when they fall.

And use rate checking. DAT RateView shows the average on a lane over the last thirty days — compare every offer against the market.

Now the red flags. Signs of a load you should question.

One: the rate is too high. If it sits thirty to forty percent above market, ask why. The freight may be hazardous or desperately urgent, or the broker may be unreliable.

Two: an unknown broker with no rating and no reviews, and an MC number that does not check out. Demand payment up front or walk away.

Three: too many stops. If the rate con lists three, four, five stops, that eats your time and your profit.

Four: impossible delivery windows. Loading in California tonight, delivering in New York the morning after next — that is 3,000 miles in 36 hours. Physically impossible.

Five: the load has been sitting. If it was posted two, three, four hours ago and is still available, other dispatchers already passed on it. Find out why.

Six: the broker dodges questions. You call and ask about the freight, the rate, the details, and they deflect. Bad sign.

Seven: pressure to commit immediately. "Decide right now or it goes to someone else." That is manipulation.

Eight: terms change after booking. You book it, then the broker calls — sorry, the rate changed, or we added another stop. Unprofessional.

Nine: no rate con. The broker says "I'll send it later" or "I'll email it," and never does. No rate confirmation, no deal.

Ten: bad reviews. Check what other carriers say. If they all complain about slow payment or hidden deductions, avoid that broker.

One last tip: trust your gut. If something feels wrong, it usually is. Better to skip one load than to lose money and time on a bad one.
