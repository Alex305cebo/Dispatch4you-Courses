Real cases from practice. Three situations, solved step by step.

Case one: a breakdown on the road. Tuesday, two in the afternoon. The driver calls: the engine is overheating, I cannot keep going. The truck is on I-40 near Amarillo, Texas. The load is electronics, Dallas to Los Angeles, 1,400 miles, delivering Thursday at eight in the morning, rate $3,200.

What do you do? Step one: get the details from the driver. What exactly happened, can he make it to the next exit? Step two: find the nearest service through Google or Trucker Path, and call a mobile mechanic. Step three: tell the broker immediately. "My driver broke down, the mechanic arrives within an hour, new ETA is Thursday at noon instead of eight." Step four: stay on the repair. In this case it was a coolant leak, a hose replacement, $230, an hour and a half. Step five: driver rolling again, update the broker. Result: the load delivered on time and the broker was pleased with the communication.

Case two: the broker has not paid in sixty days. Five thousand dollars sitting there. Step one: confirm every document went out — rate confirmation, BOL, POD, invoice. Step two: send a formal demand letter requiring payment within ten business days. Step three: check the broker on FMCSA — do they still have their $75,000 surety bond? Step four: file a complaint with FMCSA. Step five: if you use factoring, they buy the invoice at ninety-five to ninety-eight percent and chase the debt themselves. Step six, last resort: small claims court, filing fee $50 to $200. In reality, after a formal letter most brokers pay within fifteen days.

Case three: the driver quits with no warning, and the truck is loaded and rolling. Friday, four in the afternoon. The driver calls: I quit, the truck is at the Flying J in Oklahoma City, keys under the mat. The load is furniture, Houston to Denver, a thousand miles, delivering Monday.

What do you do? Do not panic. Check GPS for the truck's exact location. Try to work it out with the driver — offer a $500 bonus to finish this one load. If he refuses, find a replacement fast: driver agencies, Facebook groups, drivers you know. And tell the broker honestly what happened. In this case a replacement came through an agency for $1,100 in extra cost, but the load arrived on time and the reputation held.

The lesson: always keep backup drivers.
