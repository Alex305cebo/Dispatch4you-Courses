Six key documents in freight. Each one in detail: what it is, why it exists, what to check.

Document one: the rate confirmation, or rate con. A legally binding contract between broker and carrier for one specific load. The most important document in a dispatcher's work. It locks every term of the deal: route, rate, dates, equipment type, payment terms and any extra requirements. Sign it and you agree to all of it, including the fine print at the bottom of the page. This matters: if the rate con says detention is zero dollars, you get no detention pay even if your driver sat for six hours. Read every line before signing.

Document two: the BOL, the bill of lading. The legal document confirming the carrier received the freight from the shipper. It is issued at the loading dock and is three things at once — the transport contract, the receipt for the goods, and the shipping manifest. It rides with the freight from pickup to delivery and stays in the cab the whole way. At pickup the driver must take the BOL from the shipper, count the pallets, check the weight and look for damage. If anything does not match, write it on the BOL: damage or shortage, noted before delivery. At delivery, get the receiver's signature, confirm the date and time are written in, and photograph every page with the signatures. That photograph is your POD. Without a signed BOL there is no proof of delivery, and no payment.

Document three: the POD, proof of delivery. The photograph of the signed BOL that proves the freight arrived. Without it the broker does not pay, and the factoring company will not buy your invoice either. Requirements: sharp photo, all text readable, all four corners of the document visible, the receiver's signature legible, date and time of delivery shown. Send it the same day, twenty-four hours at the latest. If the receiver refuses to sign, the driver does not leave without either a signature or a written refusal.

Document four: the invoice. The bill you send the broker after delivery. It must carry your company name and MC number, the load number from the rate con, pickup and delivery dates, the route, the agreed rate, any extra charges — detention, lumper, TONU — and your banking details or your factoring company's details. The rule for fast payment: send the invoice together with the POD in one email. The sooner the broker has the full package, the sooner the days-to-pay clock starts. Speed is money.

Document five: the carrier packet. The set of documents you send to register with a new broker. Without it the broker cannot add you to their system. It contains: a W-9 form, a certificate of insurance showing auto liability of one million dollars and cargo coverage of at least one hundred thousand, a copy of your MC authority from FMCSA, the signed broker-carrier agreement — read it carefully — an equipment list, and your banking information or a notice of assignment from your factoring company. Important: a carrier packet contains sensitive data. Send it only to brokers you have verified. Scammers collect carrier packets for identity theft.

Document six: the supporting paperwork. The lumper receipt, for unloading services at the big retail warehouses — Walmart, Costco, Target. The driver pays fifty to three hundred dollars on the spot, gets a receipt, and the broker reimburses it. No receipt, no reimbursement. The scale ticket, from weighing at a scale house. The detention receipt, which records waiting time — without it, collecting detention pay is nearly impossible. The damage report, also called OS and D, over, short and damaged, filled out when freight arrives damaged or short, and critical for insurance claims. And the TONU letter: if a load is cancelled after dispatch, send the broker a written demand for compensation.
