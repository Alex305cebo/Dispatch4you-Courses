The main equipment types. Six kinds of trailer cover ninety-five percent of all freight moved in the United States. Here is each one, with its specs and the loads it carries.

Dry van. The most common type by far — seventy percent of all freight. A standard enclosed trailer, 53 feet long. It protects cargo from weather and theft, and it carries packaged goods: electronics, clothing, food that does not need temperature control, furniture, appliances. Loading happens through the rear doors, sometimes through side doors. Dimensions: 53 by 8 and a half by 9 feet. Weight up to 45,000 pounds. It holds 26 pallets. Rate per mile: two to two and a half dollars.

Reefer, short for refrigerated trailer. Temperature control and premium rates. It is an insulated trailer with a cooling and heating unit that holds anywhere from minus twenty to plus seventy degrees Fahrenheit. It carries perishables: fruit, vegetables, meat, dairy, pharmaceuticals, flowers. Rates run twenty to forty percent above dry van, because the equipment costs more and the reefer unit burns fuel. Dimensions: 53 by 8 and a half by 8 and a half feet. Weight up to 43,000 pounds. Rate per mile: two and a half to three and a half dollars.

Flatbed. An open deck for oversized freight. No walls, no roof. It carries what will not fit in an enclosed trailer: building materials like lumber and steel, machinery, pipe, concrete blocks. It requires tarping — covering the load — and strapping to secure it. Flatbed drivers earn more, because the work is physically harder. Dimensions: 48 to 53 feet by 8 and a half feet. Weight up to 48,000 pounds, with cargo height up to 8 and a half feet. Rate per mile: two and a half to three and a half dollars.
