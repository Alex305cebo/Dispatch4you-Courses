A typical day. A dispatcher's day starts early and demands constant attention. Here is what it looks like for an experienced dispatcher running five to seven trucks.

Six in the morning: the check-in. You reach every driver — where they are, how they slept, whether they are ready to run. You check ETA to the pickup through GPS tracking.

Seven: analysis and planning. You open the TMS, look at every truck's status, see who frees up today and where they will be empty.

Eight: searching the load boards. DAT Power, Truckstop, 123Loadboard. You filter by location, equipment type, and rates of $2.20 a mile and up.

Nine: calls to brokers and rate negotiation. The target is forty to sixty calls a day. You haggle, trying to pull ten to twenty cents a mile more.

Eleven: booking. You send the broker your documents, receive the rate confirmation, and check every detail on it.

One in the afternoon: check calls. You update brokers — the driver is two hours from delivery, the driver is loaded, here is the BOL number.

Three: problem solving. Traffic, breakdowns, delays at warehouses. Every day something goes off plan.

Five: paperwork. Drivers send PODs after unloading. You check them and prepare invoices.

Seven in the evening: the day's report. How many loads booked, what revenue, what problems came up.

Eight: end of day. But the phone stays on for emergencies.

This is not a nine-to-five. The average day runs ten to twelve hours. With experience you learn to streamline the process and automate the routine.
