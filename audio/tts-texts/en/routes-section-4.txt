Lanes. A lane is a route between two points. Some are reliably profitable, others are seasonal. The key concept is headhaul — the direction with high demand — versus backhaul, the return direction where demand is low.

Strong lanes. Chicago, Illinois to Atlanta, Georgia: 716 miles at $2.60 a mile, moving consumer goods and e-commerce. Atlanta to Miami, Florida: 662 miles at $2.20 a mile, retail and building materials for Florida's growing market.

Seasonal lanes, which peak at certain times. Miami to New York: 1,280 miles at $3.00 a mile during produce season, January through April, hauling Florida fruit and vegetables. Salinas, California to Chicago: 2,172 miles at $3.20 a mile, produce from April through October — lettuce, strawberries, broccoli. Fresno, California to Dallas: 1,500 miles at $2.90 a mile on reefer, May through September, fruit and nuts out of the Central Valley.

Backhaul lanes, where rates are low. Dallas to Los Angeles: 1,435 miles at $1.60 a mile, because demand is thin — everyone is trying to leave California, not enter it. New York to Miami: 1,270 miles at $1.50 a mile, little southbound freight. Phoenix to Los Angeles: 370 miles at $1.40 a mile, a classic deadhead stretch.

The rule for dispatchers: outbound from California, Florida and Texas is usually headhaul with strong rates. Inbound to those states is backhaul with weak ones. Plan routes to maximize headhaul miles and minimize backhaul.

Now time zones. The continental U.S. has four, and this matters more than beginners expect. Pickup and delivery times are always given in local time. If you work in Eastern and your driver is in Pacific, that is a three-hour gap. A time zone mistake can cost you the load and your reputation.

Eastern time: New York, Florida, Georgia, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Virginia, the Carolinas, Michigan, part of Indiana. Most brokers and shippers run on Eastern, and the load boards refresh there first. UTC minus five, or minus four in summer.

Central time: Texas, Illinois, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Missouri, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Tennessee, Arkansas, Oklahoma, Kansas, Iowa, Nebraska. Chicago and Dallas are the key hubs. One hour behind Eastern. UTC minus six, or minus five in summer.

Mountain time: Colorado, Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, Montana, Wyoming, Idaho. Arizona does not observe daylight saving, except the Navajo Nation. Two hours behind Eastern. UTC minus seven, or minus six in summer.

Pacific time: California, Washington, Oregon, Nevada. The ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach run on Pacific. When it is eight in the morning in New York, it is only five in Los Angeles. Three hours behind Eastern. UTC minus eight, or minus seven in summer.

The advice: always confirm the time zone when you book. If pickup is eight in the morning Pacific and you are on Eastern, that is eleven your time. Use a time converter or an app like World Clock. A one or two hour mistake means a late pickup and a fine.
