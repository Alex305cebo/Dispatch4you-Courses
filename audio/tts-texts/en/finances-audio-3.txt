A dispatcher can earn in three ways. Each has its upside, its downside and its ceiling.

Model one: employee dispatcher. Sixteen to twenty-five dollars an hour, or $33,000 to $52,000 a year. You work for a trucking company or a dispatch service. Fixed salary, stability, no risk. The company provides the load board, the TMS and the phone, so you learn on their money. The downside is the ceiling: even the best employee dispatcher rarely clears $60,000 a year. This suits your first six to twelve months, while you build experience.

Model two: independent dispatcher. Two to three percent of gross, sometimes five or more. Thirty to sixty thousand a year and up. You work for yourself, find owner-operators, and dispatch their trucks for a percentage. The standard rate is two to three percent of the load's gross revenue. With five trucks grossing $15,000 a month each, at three percent, that is $2,250 a month, or $27,000 a year. With ten trucks, $126,000 a year. Your costs stay minimal — a load board, a phone, software. This suits an experienced dispatcher with six months or more behind them.

Model three: dispatch company owner. A hundred and thirty thousand a year and up. You hire other dispatchers to work your clients. You charge the client six percent, pay your dispatcher three and a half, and keep two and a half. With thirty trucks that is $13,500 a month, or $162,000 a year. With fifty trucks, $250,000 and beyond. This suits an entrepreneur with one to two years of experience.

One detail that matters. A dispatcher's fee in the U.S. is standardly two to three percent of the load's gross revenue — not of net profit. Spell that out clearly in the dispatch service agreement with every driver. Without a written agreement, you have no grounds to demand payment at all.
