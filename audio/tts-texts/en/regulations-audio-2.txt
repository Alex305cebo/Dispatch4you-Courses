Hours of service. The rules that matter most to a dispatcher. They set how long a driver may drive, when they must rest, and when they must stop entirely. Violations here are among the most common and most expensive in trucking: fines from $1,000 to $16,000, an out-of-service order that freezes the truck until hours reset, and CSA points that stay on the record for two years.

The key habit: before every booking, check the driver's hours through the ELD. Ask two questions. How many driving hours do you have left? And when did your fourteen-hour clock start? If the driver says six hours, do not book a seven-hour run. That sounds obvious, but this exact mistake causes eighty percent of hours-of-service violations.

The eleven-hour driving limit. A driver may drive a maximum of eleven hours after ten consecutive hours off duty. That is driving time — do not confuse it with on-duty time. Loading, unloading and waiting are on duty, but they are not driving. Example: the driver rested from eight in the evening to six in the morning, ten hours. From six, they can drive eleven hours, until five in the afternoon — but only if they spend no time on other on-duty work.

The fourteen-hour duty window. This is the most misunderstood rule. The window starts the moment the driver goes from off duty to on duty or driving. From then, they have fourteen hours, and they cannot drive past that mark even if they have only used eight of their eleven driving hours. Example: the driver starts at six in the morning. At eight in the evening, fourteen hours later, they must stop — even if they only drove six hours and spent the rest waiting to load. The fourteen-hour clock does not pause for breaks or on-duty time. It runs continuously.

The thirty-minute break rule. After eight hours of driving — driving, not on duty — the driver must take at least a thirty-minute break. It can be off duty or on duty not driving, so waiting to load counts. Without it, they cannot continue driving. A tip for dispatchers: schedule loading or unloading around the eight-hour mark. That is a free break.

The sixty and seventy hour weekly limits. Two options: sixty on-duty hours in seven consecutive days, or seventy in eight. Most carriers run seventy in eight. On duty includes everything — driving, loading, waiting, pre-trip. Example: the driver works five days at fourteen on-duty hours each, which is seventy hours. On day six they cannot work until they either take a thirty-four-hour restart or old hours roll off the eight-day window.

The thirty-four-hour restart. A driver can reset the sixty or seventy hour clock by taking thirty-four consecutive hours off duty. After that, the hours zero out and the week starts fresh. Example: the driver finishes Friday at six in the evening. Thirty-four hours off puts them at four in the morning Sunday, and from that moment they have a full seventy hours for the new week. A tip: plan restarts over weekends. The driver rests, the clock resets.
