The Interstate Highway System is the circulatory system of American freight. Four main corridors tie the country together, east to west and north to south. A dispatcher needs to know each one: which cities it connects, what freight moves on it, where the traffic sits, where the tolls are, and what the seasons do to it. Understanding the corridors is understanding how money moves in trucking.

I-95, the East Coast corridor. The busiest interstate in the East, linking every major city on the seaboard: Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Washington DC, Richmond, Jacksonville, Miami. It is the artery for consumer goods, e-commerce and Florida produce. The problems: chronic congestion around New York and Washington, high tolls — the New Jersey Turnpike runs to a hundred dollars or more for a truck — narrow stretches in Connecticut and Rhode Island, and winter snow with closures. Despite all of that, I-95 is one of the most profitable corridors, simply because of the density of people and business.

I-10, the southern corridor. The main southern route from the Atlantic to the Pacific, running through Jacksonville, Tallahassee, Mobile, New Orleans, Houston, San Antonio, El Paso, Tucson, Phoenix and Los Angeles. It is critical for cross-border freight through Laredo and El Paso, and for the oil and gas industry in Houston. What to watch: West Texas, 550 miles of near-emptiness between San Antonio and El Paso — a classic deadhead stretch. Hurricane season, June through November, can close sections along the Gulf Coast. And summer heat above 110 degrees pushes reefer units to their limit.

I-5, the West Coast corridor. The main artery of the West, connecting San Diego, Los Angeles, Bakersfield, Fresno, Sacramento, Portland and Seattle. It carries produce out of California's Central Valley, the largest agricultural region in the country, which grows more than half of all U.S. fruit and vegetables. Key sections: the Grapevine, between Los Angeles and the Central Valley, which closes for snow in winter. Siskiyou Pass on the California-Oregon line, steep and dangerous in winter. CARB compliance is mandatory throughout California. And the ports of Los Angeles, Long Beach, Seattle and Tacoma generate enormous container volume.

I-80, the northern corridor. The main northern transcontinental route, running through New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Chicago, Iowa, Nebraska, Wyoming, Utah, Nevada and into San Francisco. It is the primary coast-to-coast route through the Midwest. Critical sections: Wyoming's Elk Mountain at 8,640 feet, with brutal winds and snow that close the road several times a season. Donner Pass in the Sierra Nevada, on the California-Nevada line, with chain requirements in winter. And the Ohio Turnpike, thirty dollars or more in tolls for a truck. Despite the winter trouble, I-80 is a key corridor for intermodal and dry van freight.

Beyond the four main corridors, a dispatcher should know several more interstates that link regions and create alternate routes.

I-40, central east-west, an alternative to I-10 and I-80. It runs through Raleigh, Nashville, Memphis, Little Rock, Oklahoma City, Amarillo, Albuquerque and Flagstaff. Popular for freight from the Southeast into the Southwest and California, with fewer tolls than I-80.

I-75, the eastern north-south. The main north-south corridor in the East, through Michigan, Ohio, Kentucky, Tennessee, Georgia and Florida. It passes Detroit and the auto industry, Cincinnati, Knoxville, Atlanta and on to Tampa and Miami. Critical for Florida produce, January through April, and for the automotive supply chain.

I-35, central north-south, linking the Midwest to Texas and the Mexican border: Minneapolis, Des Moines, Kansas City, Oklahoma City, Dallas, San Antonio, Laredo. It is the key route for USMCA freight — Laredo alone handles forty percent of all trade between the United States and Mexico.

I-65, Midwest to the Gulf. It connects the Chicago area to the Gulf Coast through Indianapolis, Louisville, Nashville, Birmingham and Mobile, passing through Nashville, one of the fastest-growing logistics markets in the country.
