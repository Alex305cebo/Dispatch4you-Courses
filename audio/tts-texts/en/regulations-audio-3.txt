The split sleeper berth. A driver can divide their ten hours off into two periods: seven and three, or eight and two. The minimum is seven hours in the sleeper berth, plus at least two hours off duty or in the sleeper. Neither period counts against the fourteen-hour window, which lets the driver stretch the working day. New for 2026: FMCSA is testing pilot programs for six-four and five-five splits, for more flexibility still.

The short-haul exception. For drivers working within a 150 air-mile radius of their base who return every day: no ELD required, no record of duty status required. But the fourteen-hour window still applies, and the driver must be back at base within it. Useful for local and regional work.

Adverse driving conditions. If a driver hits unforeseen weather — snow, fog, ice — or road trouble like a crash or a closure, they may extend the driving limit by two hours, to thirteen, and the duty window by two hours, to sixteen. The condition must be genuinely unforeseen. "I knew snow was coming and drove anyway" does not qualify. The driver must note it in the ELD.

Now CSA scores. Compliance, Safety, Accountability — FMCSA's system for rating carrier safety. Every carrier gets scored across seven categories, called BASICs, based on inspection results, crashes and violations. The scale runs zero to one hundred, where zero is best. Scores above the threshold — sixty-five to eighty depending on category — trigger FMCSA intervention: warnings, targeted inspections, audits, and in serious cases shutting the company down.

Why this matters to a dispatcher. A bad CSA score means insurance costs rise twenty to fifty percent. Brokers refuse to work with you — the large ones check CSA before booking. DOT officers deliberately pull over trucks with bad scores, so you get more roadside inspections. And eventually you lose business. Check your score at ai.fmcsa.dot.gov.

A change in 2025: FMCSA is reorganizing the CSA system. The BASICs are being renamed compliance categories, violation weights are simplified, and recent violations count for more — the last twelve months weigh heaviest. The goal is fairer comparison between carriers of different sizes.

Category one: unsafe driving, threshold sixty-five percent. Speeding, reckless driving, phone use behind the wheel, improper lane changes, failure to use a seatbelt. The most expensive category, because it ties directly to crashes. Each violation adds one to ten points.

Category two: crash indicator, threshold sixty-five percent. It counts every DOT-reportable crash in the last twenty-four months, even ones where the driver was not at fault. The only ways down are time, as points age off, and DataQs challenges.

Category three: hours-of-service compliance, threshold sixty-five percent. Driving beyond limits, falsified logs, no ELD, incomplete records. The most relevant category for you — your planning directly drives the driver's compliance.

Category four: vehicle maintenance, threshold eighty percent. Truck condition — brakes, tires, lights, frame, coupling devices — found during roadside inspections. The threshold is the highest here because poor maintenance is a direct safety threat.
