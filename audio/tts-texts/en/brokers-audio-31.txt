We covered the top ten brokers and the main verification step through your factoring company. Now the other eight steps. They take five to ten minutes and can save you two to five thousand dollars on one load.

A reminder on what comes first: always check the broker through your factoring company's site — OTR Solutions, RTS Financial, Apex Capital, Thunder Funding. Find the broker check section, enter the MC number, thirty seconds and you are done. If factoring refuses to buy an invoice from that broker, that is a first-level red flag. You will usually see one of two results: approved, or not approved in red.

Step one: check the MC number on FMCSA SAFER. Go to safer.fmcsa.dot.gov, company snapshot. Enter the MC or USDOT number. Status must read active — not inactive, not "not authorized." Entity type must read broker. If the status is not active, do not work with them. And if the company has existed less than six months, treat that as elevated risk.

Step two: check the surety bond. On the same FMCSA page, the insurance and bond section. There must be an active BMC-84, which is the surety bond, or BMC-85, a trust fund, for $75,000. If the bond is missing or expired, the broker is operating illegally. Remember what that bond really means: if the broker owes twenty carriers, it splits between all of them.

Step three: check the credit score on DAT or Carrier411. DAT broker score rates from A-plus to F based on payment history. Carrier411.com is an independent platform with carrier reviews. Highway is a free alternative. The minimum acceptable rating is B. Below B, work only through factoring or quick pay.

Step four: check days to pay. Thirty days is standard. Fifteen to twenty-one is good. Thirty to thirty-five is acceptable. Forty-five or more is dangerous. If a broker cannot tell you their days to pay, that is a red flag by itself.

Step five: check the contact information. The phone number in the load posting must match the number on FMCSA. The email should be corporate — at the company's own domain — not a free Gmail, Yahoo or Outlook address. Check the address on Google Maps: is it a real office or a residential house? Fraudsters frequently use virtual addresses.

Step six: check what other carriers say. Carrier411, Highway, Google Reviews, Facebook groups like Trucking Dispatcher Community and Owner Operator Land. A single complaint may be a misunderstanding. Five or more complaints about non-payment is a serious red flag.

Step seven: compare the MC number in the posting against the rate confirmation. They must match. If they do not, that is double brokering. Any discrepancy at all — in company name, address or phone — means you decline the load.

Step eight: check the age of the company. Under six months is elevated risk. One to three years is moderate. Three years or more is usually reliable. The exception is large companies that received a new MC number after reorganizing.

These eight steps, plus step zero through factoring, are your full protection against fraud. Five to ten minutes of checking can save thousands of dollars. Do not skip a single one.
