Who brokers are, and the types you will meet.

A freight broker is a licensed intermediary between the shipper, who owns the freight, and the carrier, who moves it. A broker owns no trucks and hauls nothing themselves. Their job is to find freight from a shipper, find a suitable carrier, and arrange the delivery. For that they earn a margin — the difference between what the shipper pays and what the carrier receives. Typical margin runs twelve to twenty-five percent.

Every broker must hold an FMCSA license: an MC number, or a USDOT number with broker designation. They also need a $75,000 surety bond, which is financial protection for carriers, and a process agent in every state, form BOC-3. Without these, brokering is illegal. The license costs about $300 in FMCSA fees, plus $1,000 to $5,000 a year for the bond premium.

Understand this clearly: the bond is not insurance for you. If a broker goes bankrupt owing twenty carriers ten thousand dollars each, that $75,000 bond splits between all of them, and you collect thirty to forty cents on the dollar.

The U.S. freight brokerage market is worth about $21 billion and grows seven percent a year. More than 17,000 licensed brokers operate in the country, but the top fifty companies control eighty percent of the volume. The remaining sixteen thousand nine hundred fifty are small and mid-sized — and among them you find both excellent partners and outright fraudsters.

Now the large brokers, meaning companies above a billion dollars in annual revenue. C.H. Robinson is the largest in the world at $24 billion. TQL is second at $8 billion. XPO and RXO run about $4 billion. Coyote Logistics, owned by UPS, about $3 billion. Echo Global, $2.5 billion. J.B. Hunt, $12 billion including its own fleet.

Large brokers have thousands of employees, offices nationwide and their own TMS platforms. C.H. Robinson has operated since 1905 — 121 years — with 105,000 shippers and 73,000 carriers in their database, and their own platform, Navisphere, for booking and tracking. TQL was founded in 1997 in Cincinnati and grew aggressively, with many young agents. Coyote was bought by UPS in 2015 for $1.8 billion.

Large brokers work with the largest shippers — Walmart, Amazon, Coca-Cola, Toyota, Procter and Gamble — and those contracts give them enormous daily volume. For a carrier that means a steady flow of work: you can call them every day and find freight in almost any direction. Payment is guaranteed, because large brokers almost never fail. Service is professional: trained agents, clear processes, fast paperwork. Days to pay runs fifteen to thirty. Schneider pays in fifteen days, one of the best in the industry. C.H. Robinson and Coyote pay in twenty-one. TQL runs thirty as standard. Credit ratings are A or A-plus across the top companies, and many offer quick pay for a one to two percent fee.

But there are drawbacks. Rates run ten to fifteen percent below market, because large brokers use volume to push price down — they know they will always find a cheaper carrier. There is bureaucracy: long contracts, strict document requirements, compliance checks. The relationship is impersonal, because you are one of thousands and the agent may not remember your name. And negotiating a better rate is hard, because everything runs on their pricing.

The recommendation for beginners: start with the large brokers. Minimal risk of non-payment, steady freight, professional service. Yes, the rates are lower, but you are learning with reliable partners. Once you have experience, add mid-sized and small brokers for better rates.

Briefly on the other types. Mid-sized brokers, $100 million to $1 billion in revenue — examples are Arrive Logistics and GlobalTranz. They are the middle ground: better rates, a more personal approach, still stable. Days to pay runs twenty to thirty-five, credit A or B-plus.

Small brokers, one to twenty employees, under $50 million. Rates can be the highest available, but so is the risk: late payment, possible bankruptcy, and plenty of fraud. Work with them only through factoring. Days to pay thirty to sixty, credit B or D.

Digital brokers — Convoy, Uber Freight, Loadsmart. Automated, one-click booking, payment in one to seven days. Rates are fixed and not negotiable, so use them as a supplementary source.

Niche brokers specialize in specific freight — Allen Lund in produce, Landstar with owner-operators. If your driver runs a reefer, find produce brokers: they pay fifteen to twenty-five percent more for the specialization.
