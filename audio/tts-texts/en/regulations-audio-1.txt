Laws and regulations. U.S. trucking is one of the most heavily regulated industries there is. DOT and FMCSA control every aspect, from a driver's working hours to the condition of the tires. A dispatcher has to know these rules, because the dispatcher is the one planning routes and times. An hours-of-service violation caused by a dispatcher means fines up to $16,000 and a lost authority.

Why does the dispatcher carry this? You are not behind the wheel, but you bear direct responsibility for compliance. You plan the route. You set the pickup and delivery times. You decide whether a driver can take a load or does not have the hours for it. If you book a load that cannot be delivered without breaking hours of service, that is on you.

The coercion rule, 49 CFR 390.6. FMCSA can fine a dispatch company or a broker for pressuring a driver into violating the rules. If a driver says "I don't have the hours" and the dispatcher answers "drive anyway or you lose the job," that is coercion. The fine reaches $16,000 per incident, and the driver can file a complaint with FMCSA against you personally. This is not theory — FMCSA actively pursues these cases.

What a dispatcher must know: hours of service, so you never plan an impossible route. CSA scores, to understand a carrier's standing. ELDs, so you can read a driver's logs. Insurance, so you know what is covered when something happens. Fines, so you know the price of a mistake. State weight limits, so you never send an overloaded truck. And hazmat rules, if you move dangerous goods.

A practical example. Your driver delivered in Atlanta at two in the afternoon and has three driving hours left. You find a load, Atlanta to Charlotte, 250 miles, about four and a half hours. Book it and the driver cannot deliver without breaking hours of service. That is your responsibility. The right move: find a load of 150 miles or less, or schedule delivery for the next day.

Now, who regulates this industry. DOT, the Department of Transportation, is the federal transportation ministry. It oversees every mode — aviation, rail, maritime and road — and sets general safety and infrastructure standards. Every commercial carrier must hold a USDOT number, the unique identifier used for safety tracking and audits.

FMCSA, the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, is the division of DOT that specifically regulates freight and bus transport. FMCSA is the police of the trucking industry. They issue authorities — MC numbers, broker authority. They set hours-of-service rules. They enforce ELD compliance, run inspections, maintain the CSA score database, investigate crashes and penalize violators.

How inspections work. DOT officers run roadside inspections at scale houses and on the road, at six levels. Level one: full check of driver and truck, forty-five to sixty minutes. Level two: walk-around — driver plus external vehicle inspection, thirty minutes. Level three: driver only — documents, hours of service, drug testing, fifteen minutes. Level four: a special inspection. Level five: truck only, without the driver. Level six: hazmat. Every inspection result feeds into the carrier's CSA score.
