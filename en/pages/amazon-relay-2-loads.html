4. The kinds of loads in Relay

What it is

There are four ways to get freight in Relay. The first two are open to you the day you are approved. The other two unlock once the company has a decent score. We will go through them in order, starting with the simplest.

A load from the board

Amazon posts loads in a list that looks like an ordinary load board. You see where the run starts and ends, when to pick up and what it pays. The price is final: fuel surcharge and tolls are already inside. Press Book now and the load is yours, with no phone call and no rate confirmation in your inbox. Some loads carry a Bid button instead: you name your own price and Amazon picks the best offer by the deadline. Backing out of a booked load costs you score either way.

Post A Truck: your truck on the board

This one works the other way round: instead of hunting for a load, you advertise the truck. You state where it is, when it frees up, the trailer type, solo or team, and most importantly your minimum rate per mile. The system then finds a matching load and books it for you. Remember that it books without asking, and that booking is already a commitment.

A block: you are selling time, not hauling a load

A block is not a load, it is time. You sell Amazon, say, thirteen hours of truck time on Saturday. What gets hauled in those hours is Amazon's decision, shown to the driver in the app, usually two to four short runs between facilities. You are paid for all thirteen hours even if the freight only filled eight. Drivers call the chain of runs inside a block a "tour", but that word appears nowhere in Amazon's own documents.

Auctions and contracts

Blocks are sold at auction. Amazon posts, for example, five thirteen-hour blocks in the Dallas area for six weeks out. Carriers name their price per block. The winner is not simply the cheapest bid: it is the combination of price and the carrier's performance score. A won auction becomes a contract: the same blocks on the same lanes every week. For a small carrier this is the most comfortable arrangement, because the driver knows his schedule a month ahead and the money is steady. Auctions open at grade C+, contracts at grade B.

Which one fits your situation

Take a load from the boardOpen the board, filter by area and trailer type, work out the dollars per hour for the whole loop, hit Book and assign the driver in the same minute. If the pickup is far from the truck, look at the return load before you book so you are not running empty back.
Post the truckList the truck: location, available from 2 pm, trailer type, your minimum per mile. The system books for you. Set the minimum honestly, because an accepted match cannot be dropped without a score hit. Check the Trips section first thing in the morning, a load can land overnight.
Bid on block auctionsYou need grade C+, which arrives after your first ten loads if you run them properly. Find blocks in your area, price a block against the worst case, and bid. Start with thirteen-hour day blocks so the driver sleeps at home.
Go for a contractYou need grade B. Win an auction for four to ten weeks and the blocks repeat weekly. Line up a back-up driver: dropping a contract block inside twenty-four hours costs four times the normal penalty.
Remember The board and Post A Truck are open immediately. A block is bought time, not freight, and the whole block is paid. A contract is the same blocks every week, won at auction, and it needs score.

5. How a dispatcher takes a load

What it is

Taking a load in Relay is three moves in a row: read the card, do the math, press the button and assign a driver immediately. That last word matters. A booked load with no driver on it gets pulled by Amazon after a while and counted as your rejection. So the rule is: do not book unless there is a driver who can actually run it right now.

Working out whether it is worth it

The dollars per mile on the card are misleading. $412 for 38 miles looks like almost eleven dollars a mile, but the truck actually covers more: 17 miles to the pickup, 38 loaded and 20 back to the yard, so 75 miles. Now the clock: 40 minutes out, half an hour at the first facility, an hour and twenty on the road, half an hour at the second and 40 minutes home. Roughly three hours forty. That truck earned about $110 an hour, which for a short night run is excellent.

The formula worth memorising: rate divided by every mile including empty ones, and separately rate divided by every hour including time in the yard. The second number is the one that matters. A load at $1,850 for 900 miles with 120 miles of deadhead works out near $95 an hour once the driver's sleep is counted, which is far less obvious than it looks on the board.

Booking and assigning

Book now

The load lands in Trips. No confirmation email to wait for.

Assign a driver and tractor

Trips → the trip → Assign driver. Only drivers in Active status appear. He gets a push notification with the trip.

Check the tractor number

The plate and state in Relay must match the real truck. Cameras read plates at the gate and a mismatch means no entry.

Look at the return load

Right after booking the board offers loads out of the delivery point. That is the cheapest way to kill an empty run home.

Send the driver a plan

The app gives him addresses and times but not your thinking: where to stage, what to photograph, who to call. A short message saves two phone calls at 2 am.

A message that works "Sergei, Thursday run. CDW5 in Carteret, window 22:00–23:00. Get there by 21:15, stage on the lot to the right of the entrance, do not pull to the gate before 21:45. The moment you are in the gate line, tap Check In. Amazon trailer, drop and hook. Do the trailer inspection in the app: photos all round, seal, tires. If the seal number does not match the paperwork, do not leave, call me. Then JFK8 on Staten Island by 02:30, tolls are in the rate. I am on all night."

Things you must not do

⏳

Book "just in case"

A load with no driver gets pulled and recorded as your rejection.

🔁

Put one truck on two overlapping trips

You will end up cancelling one of them at the full penalty. Keep a truck calendar outside Relay.

💬

Bid on a load you cannot run

An accepted bid is exactly as binding as Book now.

📞

Ask support to raise the rate

There is no such mechanism in Relay.

Remember Count dollars per hour for the whole loop, not the per-mile figure on the card. Book only with a driver in hand and assign him immediately. Check the tractor number. Look at the return load while you are booking.

6. The driver at the facility, and what you do meanwhile

What it is

An Amazon facility is not a warehouse where a guard writes your plate in a notebook. The gate opens on a code from the app, instructions appear on screens and in the phone, and sensors on the way out check that the right trailer is leaving. Every tap the driver makes leaves a timestamp. Amazon scores your on-time performance from those timestamps, and you prove your detention with the very same ones. So your main job here is making sure the driver taps at the right moments.

Walk the facility step by step

Use Next, or click a step number. The diagram highlights where the driver is. On the left is what he does, on the right what you do at that moment.

DriverApproach: thirty to forty-five minutes early. He waits on the approved staging lot without blocking the entrance. Tractor and trailer are already entered in the app, so the gate pass with its QR code is ready. Some facilities will not let a truck in early.
DispatcherYou can see on the map that the truck is close. Remind him: the moment you reach the gate, tap Check In.
DriverGate: tap Check In. He shows the QR code to the scanner, or the camera reads the plate. At that same moment he taps Check In. This timestamp is what decides whether you were on time. Arrive at 22:05 and tap at 22:40 after talking to the guard, and Amazon records a 35-minute late arrival.
DispatcherCheck the timestamp in the trip timeline. If he forgot to tap, call immediately.
DriverYard: find your door or slot. The screen at the entrance or the app gives a dock door for a live load, or a slot number where the trailer sits. Drive the marked route, usually five to ten miles per hour, and never park in someone else's slot. Yards are on camera.
DispatcherIf no slot or door is assigned, have him photograph the app screen and open a support case with the exact time.
DriverTrailer: drop, find, inspect. He drops the trailer he brought in the assigned slot and confirms it in the app. He finds the trailer for the next leg and verifies its number. Then the trailer inspection: photos all round, tires, lights, doors, floor, seal. A scratch he does not capture now becomes yours at the next facility.
DispatcherInsist on the inspection every single time, however tight the clock is. It is your only protection against somebody else's damage.
DriverSeal: match it and photograph it. The seal number on the trailer must match the number in the app paperwork. He never breaks a seal himself. If the numbers differ, he stays put and calls you.
DispatcherOpen the case before the truck moves. An hour of waiting is cheaper than a cargo integrity question at delivery.
DriverExit: tap Drop Off and Check Out before the gate. Sensors on the way out confirm the right trailer is leaving. The delivery paperwork is generated automatically. The taps happen before the gate, not out on the highway.
DispatcherWatch the trip status. If he is not being released, it is usually the wrong trailer number; check it in the trip.

Ten rules to give a new driver

  • 1️⃣Tap Check In when you join the gate line, not after▼

    On-time is measured from that tap. Arriving at 22:05 and tapping at 22:40 reads as 35 minutes late even though the window runs to 23:00. Pull up, tap.

  • 2️⃣App open and location on from Begin work to the last Drop Off▼

    Half of the app-usage metric is location availability. Close the app at a truck stop or let the phone die and the score drops. Charger in the cab, battery saver off for Relay.

  • 3️⃣Always do the trailer inspection, even in a hurry▼

    All six sides, tires, lights, doors, floor, seal. Damage you do not photograph now belongs to you later. The inspection is your insurance, not Amazon's paperwork.

  • 4️⃣Verify the trailer number three times: app, trailer, paperwork▼

    Similar numbers in neighbouring slots are routine. Take the wrong trailer and the exit sensors stop you, and if they do not, you have an incident and a phone call from Amazon.

  • 5️⃣Never break a seal, photograph the number close up▼

    If it does not match the paperwork, stay where you are and call the dispatcher. Leave with the wrong seal and the cargo integrity question lands on you.

  • 6️⃣Take the selfie yourself, right away▼

    The app matches your face against the verified driver. Trying to swap drivers blocks the driver's account and creates a problem for the carrier, up to suspension.

  • 7️⃣In the yard: posted speed, your own slot, phone down▼

    Amazon yards are on camera. A yard-rules complaint lands on the carrier, and a repeat can get you barred from that facility.

  • 8️⃣Any wait over thirty minutes: message the dispatcher with the time and a screenshot▼

    Not two hours later when you are angry, but at the thirty-minute mark. The dispatcher needs to open the case while the situation is still live; a claim filed afterwards is much weaker.

  • 9️⃣No slot, no empty trailer, gate closed: do not improvise, call▼

    Improvising, like dropping a trailer by the fence and leaving, turns into an incident. The dispatcher gets an instruction from support and you follow it. The waiting time gets logged.

  • 🔟Tap Drop Off and Check Out before you roll out of the gate▼

    Leaving without checking out leaves the trip open, can block the next one and delays the proof of delivery. Driving back to press a button is the worst outcome of the day.

Watching the run

The site has a map with every truck on it. The dot refreshes about every thirty seconds from the driver's app location. If the app is closed or location is off, there is no dot and Amazon treats the carrier as untracked. A trip that is projected to run late turns red, which is your cue to call the driver and open a case in advance rather than after the fact. You may use your own ELD, but it does not replace the app: Amazon only counts the app.

Where to write when something is wrong

Amazon has no "guy you know" to call. It has round-the-clock support, reached three ways.

  • Support Center on the site. You open a case, pick a category, and the thread is saved. This is for everything that is not on fire: disputes, payment questions, appeals, documents.
  • Relay Assistant in the app. A chat that answers common questions and opens a case for the driver. Faster than a call when he is standing in a yard.
  • A live operator. Escalated from the chat or the case when the trip is at risk right now: a breakdown, a driver who cannot get in, a trailer that is not there.

How to write a case that gets answered

Trip ID and load ID Driver name and tractor number Facility code and which stop it is What happened, with exact times: "checked in 02:21, sent to slot D-114 at 02:34, slot empty, yard worker confirmed no empties at 02:50, still waiting, now 03:40" What you are asking for: an instruction for the driver, detention pay, a new appointment Attachments: screenshot of the app instruction, photo of the empty slot, the yard worker's name

From real life A driver sat three hours on a live load. The dispatcher remembered it the next day and wrote "we waited a long time yesterday". Detention was denied. The second time, the same dispatcher wrote after ninety minutes, attached the Check In screenshot and the door time, then added the departure time after the truck left. Detention was paid.
Remember Check In goes in the moment the truck reaches the gate. App open and location on for the whole trip. Trailer inspection and a seal photo every time. On any problem the driver stops and calls, and the dispatcher writes with exact times.
